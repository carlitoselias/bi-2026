Evening ticket demand

03
Storytelling · Foundations

What should the director hear first?

Evening demand exceeded capacity in the two weeks after the event. Review evening capacity before the next event.

Why lead with evenings?

Ticket demand rose 18% overall. The evening shortfall tells the director where to act.

How many extra places?

The case does not say how many requests went unfilled. Count those requests by evening before recommending a number.

From visual system to storytelling

Two complementary layers

A shared visual language

The visual system gives a presentation a consistent grammar.

A specific message

Storytelling determines meaning: what matters in this case and how to interpret it.

The two layers work together. A strong visual system alone can produce an attractive presentation with no clear message. A strong narrative without a coherent visual system can be difficult to read.

What changes for the reader?

A reader can understand every chart in a report and still wonder which comparison matters.

The director needs to know when to add places. The overall rise gives context. The evening comparison tells the director when capacity fell short.

The reader has limited cognitive capacity

Working memory is limited

Problem

Too much information competes for attention.

Limitation

Working memory holds only a small number of items; the source uses a rough range of 4–7.

Response

Provide a clear narrative guide.

Benefit

Reduce the load on the reader.

Without a clear narrative guide, a dense chart with many series, colors and labels competes with itself for attention.

How should we interpret “4–7 items”?

Treat it as a rough teaching guide, not a fixed design limit. Capacity depends on the task and how information is grouped. Cowan reviews evidence for about three to five chunks when grouping and rehearsal are controlled. Cowan (2001) ↗

Storytelling · Foundations

The core elements of storytelling

Who is the audience?

Their needs, prior knowledge, decision context and preferred language.

What is the central message?

The main idea, expressed in one concrete statement that can be evaluated.

What evidence supports it?

The data, comparisons and analyses that support the message.

These three elements must work together. Answer these questions before designing the first visualization.

The audience shapes the narrative

18% more tickets were requested in the two weeks after a cultural event. Evening demand exceeded capacity.
Venue director

“Evening demand exceeded capacity in the two weeks after the event. Review evening capacity before the next event.”

Finance lead

“The 18% rise tells us demand grew. To decide whether extra evening places would pay for themselves, compare the cost with the revenue from tickets we could actually sell.”

Data analyst

“The 18% is for all requests across the two weeks. Match evening requests to available places by session and report how many requests went unfilled.”

Evidence that supports the story

Data do more than illustrate a narrative: they test it and give it credibility. A data story needs quantitative support; evidence also needs a narrative that makes its meaning clear.

Illustrative patterns

Comparisons

Before and after; group A and group B; actual and expected.

Breaks

Abrupt changes, anomalies and critical events.

Trends

Behavior sustained over time.

Magnitude

Absolute and relative sizes of the phenomenon.

Narrative focus

“A chart needs a protagonist. If everything stands out, nothing stands out.”

An effective narrative requires choices about what to emphasize, what to retain as context and what to leave out. This does not mean hiding critical information. It means deliberately prioritizing what matters for this particular story.

For a city council, show the change in total emissions first. For a transport manager, open with transport emissions and use the city total as context.

Storytelling · Foundations

What ethical storytelling is not

Cherry-picking only favorable data.

Ending at the peak hides the decline.

Manipulating axes to exaggerate differences.

A baseline of 50 makes 51 and 53 look far apart.

Omitting critical context.

Thirty requests out of how many places?

Presenting correlation as causation.

Two rising lines alone cannot show that one caused the other.

Distorting the magnitude of a phenomenon.

Doubling a circle’s radius makes its area four times larger.

14
Storytelling · Foundations

What ethical storytelling does

Prioritizes relevant evidence without hiding inconvenient results.

Show the decline after the initial rise.

Simplifies complexity while preserving accuracy.

Start both bars at zero.

Interprets data without forcing conclusions.

Report the rise. Its cause is still open.

Makes methodological limitations transparent.

Leave a gap where records are missing.

Remains faithful to the actual behavior of the data.

A value of 20 gets twice the height of 10.

Can a coherent story establish a cause?

A clear sequence can make an explanation sound convincing. To claim a cause, you also need evidence that distinguishes that explanation from plausible alternatives.

A common mistake: story without data

“The event was a hit. Let’s add seats.” A busy foyer may be memorable, but it cannot tell the director how demand changed or when seats ran out.

Ticket requests rose 18% in two weeks, and evening demand exceeded capacity. That finding supports a review of evening places.

Response: support each analytical claim with verifiable evidence. Technical rigor and narrative clarity must work together.

16
Storytelling · Foundations

Methodological transparency

What would let another analyst check the ticket-demand finding?

Data source
Request records would count people who tried to book. A sales ledger would miss those who could not buy a ticket.
Extraction date
The case covers the two weeks after the event. The extraction date and comparison dates are unspecified.
Analysis method
Divide the change in requests by the reference-period total. An index of 100 in that period becomes 118 after the event.
Critical assumptions
The comparison assumes both periods count repeat requests in the same way.
Known limitations
The evening shortfall is known. The number of unfilled requests and the effect of the event are still unknown.

Storytelling · Foundations

A common mistake: too many charts

Many visualizations without a coherent thread create cognitive confusion. The audience does not know how one connects to the next or what the combined message is.

Each additional chart adds to the reading load. Without a clear narrative connecting them, the collection can produce less understanding than a single well-designed chart.

Response: fewer charts, a stronger narrative. Every visualization needs a specific role in the overall story. If it has no role, remove it.
